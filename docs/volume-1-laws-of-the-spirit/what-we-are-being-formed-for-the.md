# What We Are Being Formed For: The Kingdom of God Now

I am putting this chapter at the front of the volume because everything that follows can be read in two completely different ways, and the difference is decided here.

## The kingdom is present-tense
Jesus’s first preaching in the synoptic gospels is about the kingdom. Mark records it: “The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel” (Mark 1:15, ESV). Matthew gives the same opening words: “From that time Jesus began to preach, saying, ‘Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand’” (Matt. 4:17, ESV). The kingdom is announced as something at hand — present, breaking in, near enough that the right human response is repentance, which is to say the turn of the heart toward what is actually here.

Luke is even more explicit. When the Pharisees asked when the kingdom would come, Jesus answered: “The kingdom of God is in the midst of you” (Luke 17:21, ESV). Not future, not somewhere else, in the midst of them as they spoke. Paul, writing to a community two decades into the Christian movement, describes the kingdom in functional terms: “the kingdom of God is not a matter of eating and drinking but of righteousness and peace and joy in the Holy Spirit” (Rom. 14:17, ESV). The kingdom is a manner of life characterized by certain things — right ordering, peace, Spirit-given joy. Paul also names it as the present location of the believer: “He has delivered us from the domain of darkness and transferred us to the kingdom of his beloved Son” (Col. 1:13, ESV). Transferred — past tense. We are now in the kingdom.

What this means for the work this volume describes: the disciple — and that is what every reader of this work who has trusted Christ actually is — is being formed for life in this kingdom, now. Not waiting for it. Not preparing primarily for an afterlife state. Walking, today, in the reign of God that is actually present and that is drawing the disciple into it. The whole movement of the New Testament from Christ’s first preaching to Paul’s last letters is the announcement and explication of this present-tense kingdom.

## Vision, Intention, Means
Across the New Testament, the pattern of formation has three elements that the broader Christian formation tradition — Dallas Willard’s articulation in The Divine Conspiracy and Renovation of the Heart is the clearest contemporary statement, but the elements are older than him and rooted in scripture — has named Vision, Intention, and Means.

Vision is the seeing of what God is actually doing. Paul prays for this seeing explicitly in Ephesians: “having the eyes of your hearts enlightened, that you may know what is the hope to which he has called you, what are the riches of his glorious inheritance in the saints, and what is the immeasurable greatness of his power toward us who believe” (Eph. 1:18–19, ESV). Jesus names the same faculty in the Sermon on the Mount: “The eye is the lamp of the body. So, if your eye is healthy, your whole body will be full of light” (Matt. 6:22, ESV). Vision is not optimism, and it is not theology held at arm’s length. It is the actual seeing of the kingdom that is at hand — the recognition that the reign of God is operative in the present world and that the believer is drawn into it. Without Vision, the formation work goes nowhere because there is no horizon for it to be moving toward.

Intention is the disciple’s choice to live in what Vision sees. Joshua named it at Shechem: “Choose this day whom you will serve… But as for me and my house, we will serve the Lord” (Josh. 24:15, ESV). Jesus, in operational terms: “If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross daily and follow me” (Luke 9:23, ESV). Daily. The renewed, ongoing choice — not a single decision at conversion that exempts the disciple from continuing decisions. Paul names it as continuing presentation: “Present yourselves to God as those who have been brought from death to life, and your members to God as instruments for righteousness” (Rom. 6:13, ESV). Without Intention, Vision becomes mere theology — a description of a kingdom the disciple admires from outside.

Means is the practice by which the disciple acquires the character that makes kingdom-life sustainable. Paul names the discipline in Timothy: “Train yourself for godliness; for while bodily training is of some value, godliness is of value in every way” (1 Tim. 4:7–8, ESV). Peter gives the formative sequence in his second letter: “make every effort to supplement your faith with virtue, and virtue with knowledge, and knowledge with self-control, and self-control with steadfastness, and steadfastness with godliness, and godliness with brotherly affection, and brotherly affection with love” (2 Pet. 1:5–7, ESV). The disciplines and operational laws by which the disciple actually walks the apprentice life are the Means.

This volume is part of the Means. The thirty-eight Foundational Laws name the operational realities the disciple walks in. The Explorations are the working investigations of how those laws operate in lived life. The Periodic Table in Vol 5 is a way of seeing the structural relationships among them. Volume 2 names the diagnostic and tool-application discipline by which the Means are applied to the specific knots a disciple discovers in herself. The Fellowship of the Heart curriculum is how the Means are walked in community over a sustained period.

But the Means cannot do their work without Vision and Intention in place. A reader who walks the Means without the Vision-and-Intention frame will get formation that is structurally clinical — wound-clearing, sin-clearing, idol-dethroning — all of it real and necessary, but with no kingdom for it to issue out into. The same content, two utterly different orientations. The kingdom-horizon framing decides which one the reader actually receives.

## The apprentice’s posture
The New Testament’s primary word for the disciple is mathetes — apprentice, learner. The disciple is not a patient receiving treatment. The disciple is an apprentice receiving formation as the kind of person who can live the kind of life Jesus modeled and taught.

Jesus’s invitation in Matthew 11 is the clearest pastoral statement of this: “Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light” (Matt. 11:28–30, ESV). Three movements: come, take, learn. The yoke is the apprentice’s tool — the harness paired with the master craftsman’s, by which the apprentice walks alongside the master and learns the trade by doing it under his hand.

The destination is named in Luke 6:40 (ESV): “A disciple is not above his teacher, but everyone when he is fully trained will be like his teacher.” The apprentice’s formation is not in the direction of becoming an expert in spiritual things. It is in the direction of being like Jesus — in heart, mind, action, and the way of life.

The Formation Companion’s role in the operational application of this work (and in the Fellowship of the Heart curriculum) is to be a fellow apprentice walking a bit further along the same road. Not a master craftsman in their own right. The master is Christ. The Companion is the elder apprentice helping the newer apprentice see what the master is doing.

## How the rest of this volume sits under this
The rest of Volume 1 — the four foundational axioms, the inclusion criteria, the thirty-eight Foundational Laws, the eight Explorations, the Periodic Table machinery — is the structural account of the Means. The volume makes the case that the laws of the Spirit are real, knowable, and operable, and it offers a working catalog of the most-confirmed ones. All of that work is real and necessary. But it operates inside the kingdom-horizon this chapter names. The Foundational Laws are not abstract metaphysics; they are the operational laws of kingdom-life. The Explorations are the working investigations of how those laws are actually walked. The Periodic Table is a way of seeing how the laws relate to each other.

Volume 2 is the diagnostic and operational application of the Means to the specific blockages a disciple discovers in herself — wounds, sins, idols, lies, agreements, knots of all kinds. Volume 2 also operates inside this same kingdom-horizon: the work it describes is the apprentice’s work, not the patient’s. The participant is being formed for kingdom-life, not merely cleared so she can resume the life she was already trying to live.

A reader who keeps the Vision-Intention-Means frame in mind through the rest of these volumes will not lose the horizon. A reader who does not will eventually drift into reading the corpus as a system rather than as an apprenticeship. The kingdom is at hand. The disciple is in it. The rest of this work is how she walks in it.

## What I Am Trying to Do in This Volume

## The Starting Question
I want to begin with a question that has driven me for over two decades: what if the way the world of the Spirit works is just as logical and discoverable as the laws of physics? Not identical to physics, but equally orderly, equally real, and equally open to investigation by someone willing to pay attention.

Jesus rebuked Nicodemus in John 3, and His rebuke was not about a lack of piety or devotion. Nicodemus was a ruler of the Jews, a serious student of scripture. The rebuke was that he should have been able to understand how things worked in the Spirit, and he didn’t. That must have stung a little. If the teacher of Israel should have known, then surely, we who have the full canon, the indwelling Holy Spirit, and two thousand years of the church’s experience, should be able to see deeper than we do.

That is the starting point. Not arrogance, actually, the opposite. It is humility in the face of how much we have been given and how little we have understood.

My initial starting point is the observation that we live a life so far short of what Jesus invites us into. A life of walking in the Spirit, of miracles in the natural, and living in intimate conversation with Him.

## The Four Foundational Principles

![](images/image-013.jpeg)

As laid out in the Intro, this volume operates under four foundational axioms: (1) Laws of the Spirit exist; (2) they are knowable; (3) they are knowable in all three “worlds” — spirit, mind and emotions, body and actions; and (4) we can operate in them. The full statement is in the Intro; what follows in this volume builds on those four principles.

A note on how I am reading scripture in what follows. The Eight Explorations use a proof-text method — I identify specific passages with explicit causal structure and draw out the logical relationships. This is appropriate for law identification. But Dallas Willard’s reading of the Sermon on the Mount in The Divine Conspiracy offers a complementary lens I want to name: he argues that Matthew 5–7 is not a collection of individual ethical demands but Jesus’s most comprehensive integrated description of how kingdom life actually works — the whole thing, held together, as a picture of the life Jesus is inviting us into. I recommend reading the Sermon on the Mount alongside the Eight Explorations as a reality check: if a proposed law does not fit coherently within the Sermon on the Mount’s overall picture of kingdom life, that tension is worth examining. And if all the laws together do not begin to describe the life the Sermon on the Mount is pointing toward, the exploration is not finished. The Sermon is the target; the laws are attempts to describe its structure.

Where I have preserved a minority dissent — an alternative reading I have declined to adopt but whose argument deserves preservation — it is carried forward alongside the core reading.

## A Note on the Inclusion Criteria
A provisional law qualifies for entry to this catalog when it satisfies four criteria: (1) it lines up with scripture; (2) the Spirit of Truth, speaking through and in confirmation of scripture, witnesses to it (John 16:13–15; 1 Cor. 2:10–14); (3) it is recognized at least partially by a community of practitioners; (4) it is testable in the natural — produces observable results in the lives of people who operate in accord with it. These four are not four parallel sources of authority. Criterion (1) is the load-bearing reference point; criteria (2), (3), and (4) operate within and in confirmation of scripture, never as independent epistemic sources that could in principle validate something scripture has not established. The Spirit speaks in scripture — the Westminster Confession’s articulation is one of the clearest statements of this, though the doctrine is older — and the Spirit’s witness in the believer’s life is the ongoing application and confirmation of what scripture has already established. The Foundational Laws section that follows tests those four against thirty-eight high-confidence laws of wide consent.

One clarifying clause is worth naming explicitly. A candidate qualifies if its causal structure generalizes beyond the originating context, even when the original is a specific commandment rather than a general principle. The testing case is the Honor-Authority pattern in Eph. 6:2 (“the first commandment with a promise,” citing Exod. 20:12). The original is a specific commandment about parents; the causal structure (honor of rightly-ordered authority — flourishing of the honorer) generalizes. This clause prevents the catalog from rejecting laws-with-promises whose causal structure is genuinely general.

## How to Read This Volume
The volume holds two kinds of material side by side. Foundational Laws are operational claims with multi-author scriptural support, broad acceptance across mainstream Christian traditions, and observable testability — the thirty-eight laws of wide consent that open this volume immediately after the Opening Exploration. Explorations are propositional claims I have come to through investigation of less-mapped territory, presented at lower confidence levels and offered for community testing. The same five-field format (Type tag, Scriptural Ground, Mechanism, Proposed Law, Certainty rationale) carries both, so what differs is the confidence and the warrant, not the form.

The Periodic Table of Spiritual Laws, consolidated in Volume 5 (see The Periodic Table of Spiritual Laws — A Summing and Organizing Reference), organizes everything by two axes: scale of operation (Period 0 Scale-Invariant through Period 5 Cosmic / Eschatological) and the dimension of the person primarily addressed (Group I Spirit through Group VI Structural Frame). Each entry carries a directionality tag — (V) Vertical between person and God, (H) Horizontal between persons, (B) Both, (I) Intra-personal — together with a provenance code and a confidence tier. The slim table cells give the at-a-glance map; the reference list immediately after the table gives the description, mirror fields, and scriptural anchors for each entry.

Each entry carries a qualitative confidence tier rather than a numeric percentage. Three tiers: **Clearly Taught** — taught directly by scripture or confirmed by community testing; **Reasonably Inferred** — reasonably inferred from scripture, still in need of community testing; **Speculative** — interesting enough to pursue, not yet solid enough to build on. Entries marked ◆ are Speculative; entries marked [ OPEN UNKNOWN ] are placeholders for laws the structure of the table suggests but have not yet been developed.
