# Supplemental Exploration: Reading the Laws as Held Tensions — A Competing-Values Lens

## Why I Am Adding This Here

A reader who has just learned what qualifies as a Foundational Law in this volume is about to encounter the catalog of thirty-eight imperative-shaped sentences. *Humble yourself. Draw near. Ask. Hear and obey. Sow and reap.* The instinct of a reader trained in directional prescriptions is to take each as a one-way command: do the named thing, and the named result follows. That reading is not wrong. But it is incomplete, and the incompleteness costs more in lived formation than it costs in textual interpretation.

I want to introduce a structural lens here — one I encountered in organizational consulting work rather than in spiritual formation, but one that turns out to map onto how many of these laws actually operate. The lens is Kim Cameron and Robert Quinn's Competing Values Framework (Cameron & Quinn, 2006), developed for diagnosing organizational culture. Cameron and Quinn's core insight is not "here are four values" but something deeper: **every operational value has a diagonal opposite that pulls against it, and the pathology of an organization or a person lives in collapsing the tension toward one pole.** Health is not the achievement of one value at the expense of its opposite. Health is the disciplined holding of paired tensions, with each tension contributing its real good without being allowed to suppress the other.

I have used this framework with leadership teams and organizations for many years. What I did not see, until recently, is how directly the same structural claim applies to the spiritual life and to the laws this volume is cataloging.

## The Move

Many of the Foundational Laws read as one-directional commands. They are written that way for good reason — the imperative form is how scripture itself states them, and the directional cause-and-effect structure is what qualifies them as foundational laws in the first place. *Draw near to God, and He will draw near to you.* That is one direction. The verb is *draw*. The actor is the person.

But the law does not operate that way in actual lived experience. The person who pursues God with frantic intensity, never quieting enough to let God close the remaining distance, is not formed by the law — they are deformed by an over-investment in their own pursuit. They are working harder at drawing near than the law actually asks of them. The opposite collapse is just as common: the person who has heard the promise that God will draw near and has settled into a passive waiting that never lifts a hand to draw near themselves. The law says both halves — *draw near, and He will draw near* — and the person who pulls one half out of tension with the other has not obeyed the law, they have substituted half of it for the whole.

This is what I mean by **held tension**. The law is operationally a tension between two paired values, with characteristic deformations on each pole. The deformations are themselves diagnostic: when you see the pursuit-as-anxiety pattern, you know which pole is being collapsed; when you see the passive-waiting pattern, you know the other. Either way, the deformation tells you what is missing, and what is missing is always the held-in-tension other half.

## Which Laws This Lens Applies To

Not every Foundational Law has this tension structure. Some are descriptive (*what is sown is what is reaped* — there is no paired opposite to hold). Some are scale-articulations without a competing-values pair. The lens applies most clearly to the **imperative, paired-value laws** — the ones written with a human action and a divine response in the same sentence.

The clearest examples among the thirty-eight, and the ones I have worked through first, are:

- **[FL.IV — Humility-Exaltation](foundational-law-iv-the-humility-exaltation-law.md):** the tension between humble action and trusting that exaltation comes from God
- **[FL.VI — Hear-and-Obey](foundational-law-vi-the-hear-and-obey.md):** the tension between receptive hearing and active doing
- **[FL.VII — Drawing-Near Reciprocity](foundational-law-vii-the-drawing-near-reciprocity.md):** the tension between human pursuit and trust in divine response
- **[FL.X — Ask-Seek-Knock](foundational-law-x-the-ask-seek-knock.md):** the tension between persistent asking and the alignment that prevents asking-as-demand

Each of these four chapters now carries a **Shadow Pair** sub-section naming both deformations explicitly. The shadow is the diagnostic. When you see the over-acted shadow in a person's life (your own included), you know the pole that has been collapsed. When you see the over-awaited shadow, you know the other. The integration the law is actually after is the held-in-tension simultaneity of both.

## What I Am Not Doing With This Lens

I am not suggesting that the named laws are wrong, or that the imperative form of scripture is misleading. The directional language in the texts is theologically primary. *Humble yourself.* That is what the text says, and what the text says comes first. The shadow-pair reading does not replace the directional reading; it sits alongside it, naming the pastoral failure modes that are common enough in lived formation to be worth naming as part of how the law operates.

I am also not suggesting that this lens is original to me, or that Cameron and Quinn's framework was the source of any spiritual insight. The insight is in the texts. The lens is a way of articulating something the texts already contain, which I had not seen as clearly until I had spent a long time using a related framework in another domain. That is how some kinds of seeing work — you carry a tool around long enough that you start to see its applications everywhere, including in territory you did not originally develop it for.

## A Note from My Own Formation

I found this framework very useful in diagnosing and advising CEOs and other leaders in my consulting practice. The tools that Cameron and Quinn offered were very helpful in recognizing both where an organization and its leadership were at the current time, and where they wanted to be. The difference between those two could then be used to define a path and identify immediate next steps. Even the diagnostic results by themselves brought clarity to the individuals involved. When I did this with an organization's leadership team, the diagnostic process often revealed things that would start conversations they had never had before, but realized they needed to have. These tools can be adapted to recognize what is really going on and make visible things that can be acted on.

That same adaptive use is what I am proposing here. The competing-values structure that helps a leadership team see the tensions their organization is actually holding is, I think, the same structure that helps a person see the tensions their formation is actually holding — and in both cases, the diagnostic value is in the making-visible. What is named can be addressed. What stays inside an undifferentiated "I should be growing spiritually" stays unaddressable.

The same structural insight ran through a related project of mine: I have spent many years developing what I call the **Balanced Scorecard Process (BSCP)** — a methodology for surfacing, measuring, and balancing the competing values that an organization is actually operating under, originally built to address the gap between contractually-met service-level agreements and visibly-unsatisfied customers in outsourcing relationships. The methodology's core move is, in its own language, what this volume's held-tensions reading is in spiritual-formation language: *the balancing is the practice*. As I wrote in an early BSCP draft, "as these metrics are built, the tension has to be maintained to keep the balance in the overall result… is productivity balanced against quality and schedule and budget commitments and measurements?" The mathematical requirement that the categories be *complete and orthogonal* — so that moving one does not automatically move another — is the operational form of the competing-values claim that there is no single value an organization can over-invest in without paying for it on the other axes.

I did not arrive at the held-tensions reading of the Foundational Laws by deductive application of Cameron and Quinn. I arrived at it because I had been doing this kind of work — in BSCP form, in client engagement after client engagement — for decades, and the spiritual-formation analogs eventually became visible because of the operational practice, not in spite of it. The forms differ. The metrics in BSCP are numerical; the diagnostics in the Foundational Laws are observational. The "vital signs report" that summarizes a client's balanced operation has, in the spiritual-formation domain, no quantitative analog yet. But the underlying structural claim — that health is a held tension between competing values, and that the deformation tells you which pole is being collapsed — is the same claim in both registers.

For the reader interested in the operational depth, the BSCP work is being published as a separate volume at [**The Balanced Scorecard Process**](https://jgtittle-ministries.github.io/balanced-scorecard-process/). The two projects sit alongside each other in my own thinking: BSCP names how to do this work for organizations, this supplemental names how to read it in the spiritual life. Each tightens the other.
