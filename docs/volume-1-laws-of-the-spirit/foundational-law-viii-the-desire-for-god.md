# Foundational Law VIII: The Desire-for-God Law

The Desire-for-God Law belongs with the Drawing-Near Reciprocity Law (FL.VII). Drawing-Near is the operational law: when a person moves toward God, He moves toward the person. But what makes a person move toward God in the first place? What is the heart actually doing when it begins the seeking that Drawing-Near describes? The Desire-for-God Law names the answer. There is a longing built into the heart that points toward God before the person knows what it is. The longing is the inward signal of being made for Him, and it is also the engine of the moving toward Him that Drawing-Near describes. Without the longing, there is no seeking; without the seeking, there is no being-drawn-near-to.

C. S. Lewis named this in Surprised by Joy — the inconsolable secret that turned out to be a pointer toward the country we were made for. Augustine named it in the opening of his Confessions — You have made us for yourself, O Lord, and our heart is restless until it rests in you. The Eastern tradition names it as eros for God in Maximus the Confessor and Gregory of Nyssa; the Western tradition as desiderium; the contemplative tradition across centuries as the engine of the inward life. The longing is the same; the names are different. Scripture names it across both Testaments by at least nine biblical authors.

## The Scriptural Ground
The longing of the human heart for God is named across both Testaments, in multiple registers and by many biblical authors. The Old Testament psalms hold the experiential heart of it; the wisdom literature names its structural form; the New Testament epistles state its theological reality; the Gospels bless it; Revelation closes scripture itself with the invitation it offers.

Eccl. 3:11 (ESV): “He has made everything beautiful in its time. Also, he has put eternity into man’s heart, yet so that he cannot find out what God has done from the beginning to the end.” The structural claim: eternity has been put into the human heart. The capacity for the infinite has been built into a finite creature. The result is a heart that is not at rest with finite things alone, because the finite is not what the heart was made for.

Ps. 42:1–2 (sons of Korah, ESV): “As a deer pants for flowing streams, so pants my soul for you, O God. My soul thirsts for God, for the living God. When shall I come and appear before God?” Ps. 63:1 (David, ESV): “O God, you are my God; earnestly I seek you; my soul thirsts for you; my flesh faints for you, as in a dry and weary land where there is no water.” The experiential register — thirst as bodily-soul ache, not abstract longing, specific orientation toward the One whose face the psalmist wants to see.

Isa. 26:9 (ESV): “My soul yearns for you in the night; my spirit within me earnestly seeks you.” The seeking arises from the yearning; the yearning is the prior condition of the seeking.

Matt. 5:6 (ESV): “Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied.” The Sermon on the Mount blesses the hunger-and-thirst; the disciple who longs is operating in the Beatitude. Phil. 3:8, 14 (ESV): “Indeed, I count everything as loss because of the surpassing worth of knowing Christ Jesus my Lord… I press on toward the goal for the prize of the upward call of God in Christ Jesus.” Paul’s longing is named, oriented, costly; the pressing-on of v. 14 is the operational expression of the longing of v. 8.

Heb. 11:13–16 (ESV): “These all died in faith, not having received the things promised, but having seen them and greeted them from afar, and having acknowledged that they were strangers and exiles on the earth… they desire a better country, that is, a heavenly one. Therefore God is not ashamed to be called their God, for he has prepared for them a city.” The longing is eschatological-directional: the heart was made for a homeland not in this world, and the awareness of being a stranger here is the inward signal of being made for there.

1 John 3:2–3 (ESV): “Beloved, we are God’s children now, and what we will be has not yet appeared; but we know that when he appears we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is. And everyone who thus hopes in him purifies himself as he is pure.” John ties the longing to the eschatological vision: we hope for the seeing, and the hope is itself purifying. The longing operates formationally.

Rev. 22:17 (ESV): “The Spirit and the Bride say, ‘Come.’ And let the one who hears say, ‘Come.’ And let the one who is thirsty come; let the one who desires take the water of life without price.” The eschatological closing of scripture is itself an invitation to the thirsty and desiring. The longing is what brings the participant to the water.

## The Mechanism
Three features of how the law operates.

First, the longing is structural — built into the heart’s design rather than acquired through experience. Eccl. 3:11 names this most directly: eternity has been put into the human heart. The infinite has been built into a creature designed to bear the image of an infinite God; the consequence is a heart that is not at rest with finite things alone, because the finite is not what it was made for.

Second, the longing is pre-conscious before it is conscious. Most participants are aware of “something missing,” “a longing I can’t pin down,” or what Lewis called “the inconsolable secret” — without identifying its object. This pre-conscious operation is itself the working of the law: the heart’s design pulling the participant toward the One the heart was made for, regardless of whether the participant has consciously turned that direction. The naming of the longing’s true Object — Christ as the Beauty the longing was for all along — is itself a developmental movement that the formation work in Vol 2 shepherds. The participant who attends to her longing finds the formation work doing its proper work.

Third, the longing’s signature distinguishes it from substitutional desires. Substitutional desires (food, sex, achievement, comfort) decrease in intensity with their object’s possession; an appetite satisfied is an appetite quieted. The longing for God increases with its object’s being known, because the One being known is infinite. The pattern is named directly in Phil. 3:8, 14 (Paul has known Christ, counts everything else as loss, and presses on for more) and Heb. 11:13–16 (the faithful saw the promises from afar, greeted them, and went on as strangers and exiles seeking the better country). The deepening rather than the diminishing is operationally diagnostic: a longing that increases with proximity is the longing for God; a longing that decreases with possession is something else.

## Why This Is a Foundational Law
Nine scriptural witnesses across at least nine biblical authors — Solomon, the sons of Korah, David, Isaiah, Matthew, Paul, the Hebrews author, John in 1 John, and John in Revelation — spanning both Testaments and several genres (wisdom, lament, prophecy, gospel, epistle, apocalyptic). The directional structure — heart was made for God; longing is evidence of it; longing is the engine of the seeking — is uncontested across mainstream Christian traditions. Augustine’s articulation in Confessions I.1 is the Western touchstone; Gregory of Nyssa and Maximus the Confessor articulate it in the Eastern eros tradition; the Reformed tradition names it as the imago Dei’s directional structure under sin’s distortion; the contemplative tradition across centuries names it as the engine of the inward life; C. S. Lewis’s Surprised by Joy and The Weight of Glory are the clearest modern statements.

Testability in the natural is well-documented. A participant whose longing for God has been awakened — named, attended to, oriented toward its true Object — operates differently from a participant whose longing is suppressed (numbed), substituted (the false-self’s counterfeit satisfactions, the territory of FL.III Heart-Throne), or theologized-away (the religious-performance pattern that doesn’t actually want God). Formation Companions know this empirically; participants who name their longing find that naming it changes its operation.

This law pairs with FL.VII Drawing-Near Reciprocity (Drawing-Near is the operational form of what the longing prompts) and with FL.III Heart-Throne (Heart-Throne names what the heart enthrones; the Desire-for-God Law names what the heart was made to want before idolatry redirected it). With FL.XIII Pure-Heart Vision the consummative arc completes: longing prompts seeking, seeking is met by God’s drawing near, drawing near purifies the heart, and the pure in heart see God — which is what the longing was for all along.

**Proposed Law (Mixed Structural-Operational, Wide Consent): The human heart, made for God, generates a longing for Him as a structural feature of its design (Eccl. 3:11). This longing precedes the participant’s awareness of its true Object, survives every substitutional attempt to fill it with something else, and operates as the inward signal that the participant was created for communion with God in Christ. The longing’s recognition and orientation toward its true Object is a developmental movement: from the unspecific inward ache, to the awareness that no creature can fill it, to the recognition of Christ as the Beauty the longing was for all along, to the longing’s deepening as the participant draws nearer (1 John 3:2–3; Phil. 3:8, 14). Operationally: the participant who attends to her longing and orients it toward its true Object finds the other Foundational Laws (Drawing-Near, Pure-Heart Vision, the rest) doing their proper work; the participant whose longing has been suppressed, substituted, or numbed finds the same laws functionally inert. The law operates at the vertical scale (tag V).**

***Certainty: Clearly Taught.**** Multi-author scriptural support is extremely strong (nine authors across both Testaments). Broad acceptance is essentially universal across mainstream Christian traditions, with differences only in the precise theological framing of how the longing relates to creation, sin, grace, and consummation — the underlying claim that the longing exists and operates is uncontested. Observable testability is well-documented in pastoral, contemplative, and modern theological literature. The remaining uncertainty is principally on the precise relationship between the longing-as-structural-feature claim and the imago-Dei doctrine, which different traditions theologize differently without affecting the law’s operational content.*
