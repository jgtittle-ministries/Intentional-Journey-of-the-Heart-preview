# B15 Pull-out Work

## What this protocol does
Pull-out Work is the corpus's core Level III specialty for embodied trauma and active flashback containment. When a participant is having or close to an active flashback — the trauma is in the body and the cognitive engagement the rest of the carpet-work toolkit presupposes is unavailable — B15 supplies the operational protocol: localize the trauma as a somatic metaphor in the body (often spike, knife, band, weight, knotted rope); use a physical prop (foam bat, knotted sheet) as the metaphor's stand-in; coach the participant to physically pull the metaphor out of their body while a role-player provides controlled resistance; close with the redemption move of offering the wound to Christ who carried it in His body on the cross (1 Pet 2:24).

B15 is theologically distinct from the rest of the cohort because its substantive theological move is the *redemption-closing **core** discipline* — without the closing offering of the wound to Christ, the protocol ends in secular catharsis with somatic relief but no formation. The Christian-frame version always closes in redemption; the secular framing's "poetic justice" ending (shoving the metaphor back into the original wounder via role-play; burning it) does not import. *The redemption is Christ's* — B15 joins the source-reversal core series at the eleventh core instance at the modality / specialty altitude.

B15 carries the cohort's most stringent competency-and-referral framing. Per Decision 12.4, the protocol is canonized with: Companion II minimum competency for *participation* (holding the room as supporting Companion; holding the prop; providing controlled resistance under the primary Companion's direction); Companion III for *primary facilitation*; explicit clinical-referral protocol when trauma intensity exceeds Companion competence. The corpus's posture is that B15 belongs in the core portfolio because the trauma-prevalent populations FotH work intends to serve need this protocol available somewhere; referral-only would leave the gap structurally open and force every active-flashback situation to clinical care that may not be immediately available or theologically appropriate. The canonization preserves the gap-filler value while protecting against mishandling through the competency floor and the explicit referral protocol.

**When to use it**

• **When a participant is in or close to an active flashback during a session.** The clinical signal: the participant has been operating in cognitive engagement and is now somatically reliving the trauma — body posture changes; breath becomes shallow or hyperventilating; eye contact may be lost or fixed elsewhere; the participant may report (or stop being able to report) that "it's happening again"; the cognitive engagement the rest of the toolkit presupposes is unavailable. This is the core use case; B15 is specifically designed for this moment.

• **For substantial trauma material that earlier carpet-work has surfaced but not yet contained.** When prior sessions have surfaced trauma material that B14 Kinesthetic Metaphor or A17 Carpet-Style Healing and Forgiveness have approached but not been able to contain (the participant has done the prior work and now carries somatically-located trauma that the prior work has made visible but not yet released), B15 may be the protocol the work needs next. The Companion's discernment about whether B15 is appropriate, and when, governs.

• **For sexual-abuse recovery work** where the trauma is embodied and somatic-first approaches are operationally necessary. Companion III competency strongly preferred for primary facilitation; Companion II participation appropriate with Companion III oversight.

• **As specifically-prepared session-component deployment.** Unlike most carpet-work protocols that may deploy as the work-component on Companion discernment during a session, B15 typically requires pre-session preparation — the Companion-team's competency check, the prop preparation, the role-player briefing, the body's preparation to hold the work. Spontaneous mid-session B15 deployment is operationally possible (when a flashback emerges unexpectedly and B15 is the operational response) but is the harder case; specifically-prepared deployment is the core default.

• **In bodies whose container competency and Companion-staffing can hold the work.** B15 requires a robust container under V2.Exp8 refined; Companion-team support per B13 (two-Companion structure strongly preferred even when the body's default is single-Companion); group-member readiness to hold the work without secondary trauma activation among other participants.

• **At Companion III competency for primary facilitation; Companion II minimum for participation.** No Level I participation in B15 deployments; the work's intensity and the protocol's specialty character require Companion competency floor at II.

**When NOT to use it**

• **When the trauma intensity exceeds Companion competence.** This is the cohort's most stringent referral consideration. When the Companion's discernment is that the participant's trauma requires licensed clinical care — the trauma is sustained, complex, dissociative, sexual-abuse-with-PTSD, or otherwise beyond the Companion's scope — the core response is referral to a licensed trauma clinician (preferably one with Christian-frame practice but, if needed, secular clinical care over no care). Referral is not failure; referral is competency. The Companion's discipline is to know when the work belongs outside the corpus's care and to refer cleanly.

• **In bodies whose container competency is not robust.** New bodies, bodies in difficult lifecycle stages, bodies with unresolved relational material — none of these can hold B15 safely. The protocol requires container competency that established mature bodies have developed across years of practice; bodies still developing container competency should not deploy B15.

• **Without two-Companion structure where the body's staffing permits.** B15 deployment strongly benefits from B13's two-Companion structure. The back Companion's tracking of the bigger picture is operationally essential when the front Companion is in close with active-flashback work; the group's mood, the role-player's care, the broader gathering's safety all require the back Companion's attention. Single-Companion deployment is operationally possible but is the harder case; the Companion's pre-deployment discernment about whether single-Companion deployment is appropriate governs.

• **In contexts where the redemption-closing ****core**** discipline cannot operationally hold.** Where the Companion is not committed to the redemption-closing as substantive theological work, or where the participant's theological context cannot receive the closing's offering-of-the-wound-to-Christ framing, B15 should not deploy. The protocol's secular form ends in catharsis; the Christian-frame version ends in redemption; without the closing, the work is operationally incomplete and may produce somatic relief that is not formation.

• **As substitute for verbal/cognitive work that is operationally available.** B15 is specifically for situations where cognitive engagement is unavailable; participants whose cognitive engagement is operationally intact should not have B15 deployed as a "deeper" form of work. The protocol's value is specifically for trauma-contained-in-body situations where verbal/cognitive entry cannot reach.

• **For substance-related somatic phenomena.** Some somatic phenomena have direct medical or substance-related causes warranting medical attention rather than carpet-work. The Companion's discernment distinguishes trauma-resident somatic material from medically-resident somatic material; medical material routes to medical care, not to B15.

## The protocol
B15 runs across six operational moves, typically 45–75 minutes for a complete deployment. The protocol moves more quickly than other carpet-work because active-flashback containment is time-sensitive and prolonged engagement compounds the participant's somatic load.

**Move 1 — Recognize and confirm active or near-flashback state; secure container (5–10 minutes)**

**Companion framing:** *"[Participant], I'm seeing that what's happening right now is in your body more than in your words. The cognitive work we were doing isn't where this lives. Are you with me? Can you hear me?"*

**Companion work:** Confirm the operational state. The flashback-recognition signals include: posture rigidity or collapse; breath changes (shallow, hyperventilating, held); eye-contact loss or fixed-gaze; reported re-experiencing ("it's happening again"; "I'm there"); somatic shaking; sweating; cold extremities; partial dissociation (the participant is operationally present but their attention is partially elsewhere). The Companion's discernment confirms B15 is the protocol the moment needs.

**Companion framing if confirmed:** *"What we're going to do is bring what's in your body out into the room where we can work with it. The Lord is here with us; we're not alone in this; we're going to do this carefully. Can you receive that?"*

**Companion-team work:** The Companion-team secures the container — front Companion in close; back Companion at the edge tracking the group; group members briefed (briefly, with the participant's awareness) on what is about to happen and how they can support. If two-Companion structure is not operationally available, the single-Companion Companion checks the container's robustness: are the participants in the room able to hold the work without being secondarily activated? Is the physical space appropriate? Is there time for the full protocol?

**Companion discernment.** Move 1's central question is *whether B15 deploys at all*. The trauma intensity may exceed Companion competence; the container may not be robust enough; the participant may need clinical care now rather than carpet-work. If the Companion's discernment is that B15 should not deploy, the response is to stabilize the participant in the room (grounding work; breath work; reorientation to the present; bringing the participant gently back from the activation), close the work-component cleanly, and route the participant to subsequent care — pastoral check-in within 24 hours; clinical referral if appropriate; specific follow-up planning. *Not deploying B15 is itself a valid clinical-pastoral decision.*

If the discernment is that B15 deploys, Move 1 closes with the participant's brief consent (*"Are you with me on this?"*) and the Companion-team's coordinated entry into the protocol.

**Move 2 — Localize the trauma as a somatic metaphor in the body (5–10 minutes)**

**Companion framing:** *"With your body holding what it's holding — where in your body is the trauma right now? What part of you is carrying it? And what does it feel like — what's the shape, what's it look like as a thing in your body?"*

**Participant work:** Localize the trauma somatically and identify the metaphor. The localizations common to trauma: chest (heart area; sternum; throat-area); gut (low and deep; sometimes radiating); back (between shoulder blades; lower back); head (specific localized pressure or pain); pelvis (sexual-abuse trauma specifically often localizes here); specific limb if the trauma was physical. The metaphors common to trauma: spike (stabbing through); knife (cutting); knotted rope (tightening); band (constricting around); weight (pressing down or in); ball of darkness; razor-wire; jagged stone.

**Companion discernment.** This move is operationally close to B14 Kinesthetic Metaphor's Move 2 but operates under trauma-containment time-pressure. The Companion paces appropriately — fast enough to maintain the work's momentum; slow enough for the participant's body to supply the metaphor. The Companion does not suggest the metaphor; the participant's body supplies it. V2.Exp7A discernment-of-voices operates throughout; the Companion attends to whether the metaphor is the participant's body's reading or whether internal-voice material (the accuser; an internalized lie about the trauma's nature) is shaping the reading.

If the localization and metaphor surface immediately (the participant has been working with this material before and the metaphor is known), the move runs quickly. If the somatic localization is unstable (the trauma is moving in the body; the metaphor keeps shifting), the Companion may need to engage stabilization work briefly before proceeding to Move 3.

**Move 3 — Introduce the physical prop as the metaphor's stand-in (3–5 minutes)**

**Companion framing:** *"What's in your body, we're going to bring out into the room. [Holds up the prop — foam bat, knotted sheet, or similar appropriate to the metaphor.] This [bat / knotted sheet / prop] is the [spike / knife / knotted rope / band / weight] for now. It's not the actual trauma; it's the placeholder so we can work with it physically. Take it from me when you're ready."*

**Participant work:** Receive the prop. The transfer is operationally meaningful — the participant takes hold of the prop and the prop is now the placeholder for the somatic metaphor. The participant may hold the prop in the location in the body where the trauma is localized (the foam bat held against the chest where the spike was; the knotted sheet wrapped around the area where the band was constricting).

**Companion discernment.** The prop choice matters operationally. Common prop selections by metaphor type: *spike, knife, sharp-pointed metaphors* → foam bat held by the pointed end so the participant can wield it; *knotted rope, band, constricting metaphors* → knotted sheet that can be tugged at and pulled; *weight, pressing-down metaphors* → cushion or weighted object the participant can lift off; *ball of darkness, dispersed metaphors* → object that can be physically removed from the body. The Companion's pre-session preparation should anticipate likely metaphor categories and have appropriate props available.

The Companion's framing names the prop as placeholder — *"it's not the actual trauma; it's the placeholder so we can work with it physically."* The placeholder framing is structurally similar to B11's figure-as-door framing; the prop is the door through which the participant can engage the trauma physically rather than the trauma itself. The framing protects against magical-thinking deployment of the prop ("if I destroy this prop, the trauma is destroyed").

**Move 4 — Pull the metaphor out of the body with controlled resistance (10–20 minutes)**

**Companion framing:** *"Now we're going to physically pull the [metaphor] out. [Role-player] is going to hold the other end of the [prop] and provide resistance — not enough to win; enough that you have to work to pull it out. You'll pull; they'll resist; you keep pulling until the [metaphor] is fully out of your body. The Lord is here with you in this; you're not alone in the pulling. Are you ready?"*

**Participant work:** Engage the physical pulling-out of the metaphor. With the role-player holding the other end of the prop and providing controlled resistance, the participant physically pulls the prop away from where the trauma is localized in the body. The pulling is somatic — the body engages the work; the participant's musculature, breath, and posture all participate. The role-player provides resistance that requires the participant's sustained engagement but does not prevent the pull-out.

The work has a felt-sense completion-point — the participant's body signals when the metaphor is fully out. The body softens; the breath changes; the somatic load shifts. The Companion attends to the completion signal; the work continues until the signal arrives.

**Companion discernment.** Move 4 is the protocol's substantive somatic work. The Companion's discernment about pacing matters substantially:

• *Role-player resistance calibration.* The resistance must be enough that the work is effortful but not so much that the participant cannot complete the pull-out. The role-player needs to read the participant's strength and effort in real-time; the back Companion (per B13) may signal to the role-player about resistance level when needed.

• *Participant somatic engagement.* The work happens through the participant's body. If the participant disengages from the somatic work mid-protocol (drops the prop; stops pulling; goes still), the Companion attends — sometimes the participant needs encouragement to continue; sometimes the participant has gone into deeper trauma activation that requires different response; sometimes the work has reached a different kind of completion than full pull-out.

• *The completion-signal recognition.* The body's signal that the metaphor is fully out is specific and recognizable to Companions with B15 experience — the somatic shift is unmistakable. New Companions may need to lean on Companion III oversight during early B15 deployments to develop the completion-signal recognition.

V2.Exp7A discernment-of-voices operates throughout Move 4. The participant's body may surface verbal material during the pull-out (cursing the original wounder; calling out for help; speaking words from the original trauma moment); the Companion attends to whether the speech is the participant's authentic somatic-emotional emergence or whether internal-voice material requires its own handling later.

Where active flashback intensifies during Move 4 rather than resolving toward completion, the Companion's discernment may shift: the work may need to close incompletely with grounding-and-stabilization work (rather than forcing completion); the participant may need post-session pastoral check-in and possible clinical referral; subsequent B15 deployment may need to wait until additional preparation is in place.

**Move 5 — Redemption-closing: offer the wound to Christ (10–20 minutes)**

**Companion framing:** *"What just came out of your body — that wound that's been carried in your body — Christ carried in His body on the cross. *He Himself bore our sins in His body on the cross... for by His wounds you were healed* — 1 Peter 2:24. What you just pulled out of your body, you offer to Him. He has already carried it. Hold out what you're holding [the prop, still in the participant's hand]. Let me lead you in offering it to Him."*

**Participant work:** Engage the redemption-closing. The participant, with the prop still in hand, offers the wound to Christ. The Companion's framing leads:

*"Lord Jesus, I bring to You what You have already carried. This [pause as participant names the trauma material somatically: 'this fear I've held in my body since I was seven'; 'this shame I've carried in my chest since the assault'; 'this rage I've held in my gut since my father left'] — You carried this in Your body on the cross. I offer it to You now. You carried what I could not carry. Receive what You have already taken."*

The participant prays in their own words, with the Companion's framing as scaffolding. The prayer is not formulaic — the substantive content is the offering of the specifically-named trauma to Christ who carried it. The participant may pray briefly or extensively; the Companion paces according to the participant's somatic state.

**Closing-affirmation per A13:** Following the redemption-prayer, the Companion's closing affirmation: *"What you carried, Christ has taken. What you offered, He has already received. You are not the trauma; you are the one for whom Christ carried the trauma. The wound is Christ's; the redemption is Christ's; you are His."*

**Companion discernment.** Move 5 is the protocol's substantive theological work. Without Move 5, B15 is secular trauma-catharsis with somatic relief; with Move 5, the work operates under the source-reversal core discipline at the *redemption is Christ's* altitude. The Companion's framing is operationally constitutive — the Christ-carried-the-wound framing is not a closing prayer added to the end; it is the substantive completion of the protocol's theological work.

The participant's emotional response to Move 5 varies. Some participants experience the offering as immediate release — the somatic load that the Move 4 pull-out lifted is now offered to Christ and the participant rests in Christ's bearing of the wound. Some participants experience the offering as the beginning of substantive grief — recognizing that Christ has carried the wound for them is itself emotionally substantial and may produce tears, deep release, prolonged quiet. The Companion holds whatever response surfaces.

The prop is set aside during or after Move 5 — the Companion takes it from the participant gently; sets it aside; names that the prop is just a prop and that what was carried has been offered to Christ. The setting-aside operationalizes the prop's release from any residual identification with the trauma-material it held.

**Move 6 — De-roling and grounding to present (10–20 minutes)**

**Companion framing for de-roling per A14:** *"Now we de-role. [Role-player] held the resistance for [participant]; let's release the role." The role-player names themselves out: *"I am not the resistance; I am [their own name]."* The participant responds: *"You are not the resistance; you are [their name]. Thank you for holding the work with me."* The de-roling for the role-player who provided resistance is particularly important because the role-player has held substantive trauma-engagement weight; explicit release supports the role-player's care.*

**Companion framing for prop release:** *"This [bat / knotted sheet / prop] is just a [bat / knotted sheet]. What it held for you has been offered to Christ. Setting it down."* The prop is set in a specific location in the room (often the room's edge or in a specific corner); the prop release is structurally similar to A14 de-roling but applied to the physical prop rather than to a role-player.

**Companion framing for grounding to present:** *"Now let's bring you fully back to this room, this moment, this body. Take a breath. Feel your feet on the floor. Look around the room — who's here with you? What's the date today? Where are you in your life right now?"* The grounding work brings the participant out of the trauma-engagement state into present-moment embodied awareness. The Companion paces appropriately — too fast loses the integration; too slow loses the grounding's urgency.

**Closing of the work-component within the session liturgy.** B15's Move 6 typically operates within the larger session-liturgy closing per V2.Exp8B Component 5 — the closing-affirmation, the closing-anchor, the closing prayer, the formal release of the container all operate at the gathering-level following B15's Move 6 protocol-level closing.

**Companion discernment.** Move 6 is operationally constitutive of the work's integration. The participant has been in a substantively intense state during Moves 1–5; without robust grounding, the participant may carry the activation forward into the rest of their week in ways that compromise the work's formative outcome. The Companion's pacing through Move 6 is unhurried; the grounding takes the time it needs.

**Post-session pastoral check-in is non-negotiable.** Within 24–48 hours of any B15 deployment, the primary Companion (or Companion-team) checks in with the participant to: (a) confirm the participant has stabilized; (b) attend to any continued somatic or emotional material; (c) confirm follow-up care is in place; (d) discern whether subsequent B15 deployment or other carpet-work is appropriate in the near term or whether the body needs time for the work to integrate. The check-in is structural requirement of B15 deployment.

## Companion notes
**On the redemption-closing ****core**** discipline as the protocol's substantive theological work.** The redemption-closing is what makes B15 Christian-frame. Without it, the protocol is secular trauma-catharsis with somatic relief; with it, the protocol operates under the source-reversal core discipline at the *redemption is Christ's* altitude. The eleventh core instance of the source-reversal series operates here. The Companion's framing throughout Moves 1–4 anticipates the redemption-closing; the closing itself is substantive; without the closing's substantive operation, the protocol is operationally incomplete.

**On Companion competency floor.** Companion II minimum for participation; Companion III for primary facilitation. The protocol's intensity and specialty character require competency that develops with sustained carpet-work practice. Companion II practitioners participating in B15 deployments under Companion III oversight develop the competency that matures to Companion III over time. Level I participation in B15 is not appropriate; the work's intensity exceeds what Level I formation can hold.

**On clinical-referral protocol.** B15 has explicit referral protocol when trauma intensity exceeds Companion competence. The Companion's discernment about referral matters substantially:

• *Sustained PTSD with daily functional impact* → licensed trauma clinician (preferably Christian-frame practice; secular clinical care if needed).

• *Dissociative identity disorder or dissociative-spectrum disorders* → specialized clinical care; B15 not appropriate without integrated clinical-pastoral team.

• *Recent acute trauma (within 6 months)* → typically clinical stabilization first; B15 after clinical care has stabilized the participant.

• *Sexual-abuse trauma with complex PTSD* → Christian-frame trauma specialist preferred; B15 may operate as adjunct to clinical care rather than as primary intervention.

• *Substance-related somatic phenomena* → medical care; not B15.

• *Suicidality* → immediate clinical care; B15 not appropriate.

The Companion's referral is not failure; it is competency. Knowing when to refer is part of what makes the corpus's care safe and effective for the populations it serves.

**On the source-reversal ****core**** instance.** B15 joins the source-reversal core series at the eleventh core instance: *the redemption is Christ's*. The verbal parallelism extends across the eleven-instance series: *the affirmation is the Lord's / the mission is the Lord's / the resonance is the Lord's / the blessing is the Lord's / the renunciation is in Jesus's name / the attending is the Spirit's / the session is the Lord's / the clearing is the Lord's / the reparenting is the Father's / the body's hearing is the Spirit's / the redemption is Christ's.* The source-reversal at B15 is theologically the most consequential of the cohort's three new instances at the specialty altitude because the secular catharsis-ending would directly compromise the source-reversal at the protocol level; the redemption-closing core discipline is what makes the reversal land.

**On role-player and Companion-team care.** B15 deployment is substantively weighty for everyone in the room — the participant, the role-player providing resistance, the Companion(s), the other group members witnessing. The Companion-team's post-gathering debrief per B13 attends to everyone's care, not just the participant's. Role-players may need particular post-session check-in; the role-player has held physical resistance to the participant's trauma material and may carry residual weight. The Companion's discipline includes attending to the role-player's care alongside the participant's.

**On B15 as Level III competency development pathway.** Companion II practitioners develop Companion III competency in part through participation in B15 deployments under Companion III oversight. The participation builds the competency that eventually qualifies the Companion for primary facilitation. Tool Time gatherings (C10) and specific Companion III training settings can deploy B15 in instructional contexts; the corpus's broader Companion-formation arc includes B15 specialty development for those Companions whose formation is heading toward Level III trauma-specialty competency.

**On the corpus's posture toward trauma work generally.** The B15 canonization carries the corpus's broader posture toward trauma work — taking trauma seriously as embodied experience that the formation work attends to; refusing to bypass trauma through purely cognitive engagement; refusing also to engage trauma without theological grounding in Christ's bearing of the wound. The trauma-prevalent populations the FotH work intends to serve need both seriousness about trauma and theological grounding for the work; B15 operationalizes both at the specialty altitude.

**On secular trauma-psychology traditions the corpus does not endorse.** Levine's somatic experiencing, van der Kolk's body-keeps-the-score framework, Ogden's sensorimotor psychotherapy, Rothschild's body-remembers framework — all carry philosophical commitments about the body, the unconscious, and the locus of healing the corpus does not import. B15's operational pattern is preserved from these traditions; the philosophical anthropology is set aside under V2.Exp0 Tool Import Discipline. The Christian-frame adaptation operates on Christ-carried-the-wound grounding (1 Pet 2:24) with the redemption-closing core discipline preserving the theological boundary.

## Materials
• **Space appropriate to physical work** — the room must accommodate the participant's physical pulling-out engagement plus the role-player's positioning for controlled resistance plus the supporting Companions plus the rest of the gathering at appropriate distance. Larger rooms than typical carpet-work; movable furniture or open floor space.

• **Physical props selected per likely metaphor category.** Common kit:

- *Foam bat* (for spike, knife, sharp-pointed metaphors)

- *Knotted sheet or knotted heavy fabric* (for rope, band, constricting metaphors)

- *Cushion or weighted blanket* (for weight, pressing-down metaphors)

- *Solid object that can be physically removed* (for ball-of-darkness, dispersed metaphors)

The Companion's pre-session preparation includes the appropriate prop kit; spontaneous mid-session deployment may need to substitute with available materials.

• **A specific location for prop release after Move 5.** Often the room's edge, in a specific corner; the prop's release-location may have its own significance (set aside symbolically).

• **Post-session pastoral check-in time-slot booked** — the Companion's calendar should hold the 24–48-hour post-session check-in time before B15 deployment begins. Without scheduled follow-up, B15 should not deploy.

## Time
• **Standard B15 deployment:** 45–75 minutes total. Allocation: Move 1 (recognize and secure) 5–10 min; Move 2 (localize metaphor) 5–10 min; Move 3 (introduce prop) 3–5 min; Move 4 (pull-out with resistance) 10–20 min; Move 5 (redemption-closing) 10–20 min; Move 6 (de-role and ground) 10–20 min.

• **Compressed B15 deployment:** Not recommended. The protocol's intensity does not compress safely; truncating any move compromises the work's integration.

• **Extended B15 deployment:** 75–90 minutes when the redemption-closing requires sustained time (the participant's response to Move 5 is substantively grief-filled and the closing needs time to settle). Beyond 90 minutes, the participant's fatigue often becomes operationally limiting; the work's integration is better served by completing what can complete and routing remaining work to subsequent sessions.
