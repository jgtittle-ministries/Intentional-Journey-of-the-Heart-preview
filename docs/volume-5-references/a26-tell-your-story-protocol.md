# A26 Tell Your Story Protocol

## Tell Your Story Protocol (V2.Exp7 — Hearing Processes, with cross-ref to V2.Exp5 Connect with Self)
## What this protocol does
Tell Your Story is the foundational early-group practice in which each member tells their life-narrative to the group across one or more sessions, with the group as witness. The story is shaped by four framing questions — *Where are the footprints of God in this life? Where are the wounds? Where are the battles? Where have there been victories and losses?* — which pull autobiography into spiritual narrative and reorient the locus of the story from the participant's self-construction to the Lord's activity perceived backward. The practice is repeated across the group's life rather than performed once; each retelling produces a different story as the Spirit clarifies what the participant is being asked to see. The group's role during a telling is witness, not processing — the receiving itself is the formation work for both teller and listeners. The protocol's primary downstream effect is that subsequent group work has the autobiographical material it depends on; the secondary downstream effect is that the container's *Safe* and *Clear* conditions are formed (not merely tested) by the early story-tellings.

**When to use it**

• **As the foundational practice in the Forming phase of any new group.** Tell Your Story is normally the first major formation work a group does after the Container conditions are established (Safe, Present, Clear, Intentional per V2.Exp8) and before the group begins protocol-level inner work (carpet-style work, clearings, mission work, etc.). Sequencing convention: invitation and vetting (B21) → container-forming → Tell Your Story → other protocols.

• **At major group-lifecycle transitions** as a recurring practice: when a new member joins a mature group; when a group reorganizes or relocates; at the closing of a group's lifecycle (a *closing version* of one's story — what changed across the group's life). The multi-retelling discipline names the story as Spirit-dynamic; the recurring practice is the operational form of that theology.

• **As upstream surfacing for downstream protocols** that require the group to know each member: A24 (Blessing Receiving) requires the group to have heard the participant's wound material to know what blessing to speak; A33 (What's at Risk) requires the group to know what is genuinely at risk for this man; A17 family (BoB Carpet-Style Inner Work) and A12 (Splitting a Role) work better when the group already knows the territory the wound material sits in.

• **Level I Companion competency** for the leading. The Companion's primary moves — invitation, framing the four questions, holding the witness-discipline in the group, closing cleanly — are operationally simple. The witness-discipline itself is a competency that develops with reps; a Level I Companion can lead the protocol from this entry alone but should expect the witness-discipline to deepen across many tellings.

**When NOT to use it (failure modes and contraindications)**

• **Skipping the four-question framework.** *"Tell us about yourself"* without the framework produces autobiography — a chronological account of the participant's life with God appearing occasionally as a character. The locus-reversal the Footprints question performs is what makes this story-telling *and not* autobiography. Companion-led framing of the four questions before the telling begins is non-negotiable; the framing is what makes the practice the practice.

• **Allowing the group to drift from witness mode into processing mode while the story is being told.** The single most common failure mode. A listener offers a clarifying question that pulls the locus toward their interest; another inserts a Bible verse; a third tells a parallel story; a fourth pastorally counsels. Each of these forecloses on what the telling was opening. The Companion's discipline during the telling is to hold the room in witness mode; if a listener moves into processing, the Companion gently re-cues the listener back to receiving and the teller back to the story.

• **Pulling subsequent protocol work into the telling itself.** A man's Wounds material surfaces a specific wound; the Companion or a listener turns the telling into an empty-chair piece of inner work mid-narrative. Category error. The story-telling stays a first-person narrative practice; the subsequent inner work, where indicated, happens later in its own protocol. The Companion's discipline is to note the wound material as a candidate for subsequent work and to keep the current protocol on its own track.

• **One-and-done framing.** Treating Tell Your Story as a kickoff exercise that happens once during group-forming and then is complete. The practice is meant to recur; the multi-retelling discipline is constitutive. Groups that hear each man's story only once develop thinner group-knowledge than groups that hear each man's story multiple times across years.

• **The polished performance.** A participant tells a cleaned-up version that protects rather than reveals — typically high on Footprints and Victories, light on Wounds and Battles. The Companion's discernment is whether the polish is appropriate to where the participant is (early in trust-building, the polish is sometimes necessary self-protection and should not be pressed against) or whether the polish is the participant's habitual way of foreclosing on what the story would reveal. The Companion does not interrupt mid-story to press; the Companion notes the pattern and may, in a subsequent telling cycle, gently extend the invitation toward the under-told quadrant.

• **The trauma dump.** A participant enters dissociation, swerves into traumatic detail that exceeds group capacity, or pulls the room into a level of distress the container is not yet able to hold. The Companion's pacing discipline: invite the participant to *name the territory without entering it* — "you can say *I have wounds in this area* without telling the wound itself, if that's where you are." The named-but-not-entered territory is still recorded as part of the story; the entering, where indicated, happens in a subsequent protocol designed to hold it.

• **Cross-disclosure violations.** A participant's story includes substantial detail about another person who has not consented to that detail being in the room — a wife, a parent, a business partner. The Companion's framing in the invitation: *tell your story; name what you need to name about other people in your life, but hold their stories as theirs, not yours.* Operational form in practice: the participant can name *that* something happened with another person and *what* happened to the participant in it, but should not narrate the other person's interior state or motivations. C14 (Confidentiality Discipline) is the adjacent Companion-competency note that governs this territory more broadly.

• **Running it before the container is formed.** A group whose Safe / Present / Clear / Intentional conditions are not yet established cannot hold the material the telling surfaces. The Companion's discernment: are the four container conditions in place such that a man's story can be received without being processed, fixed, or evaluated? If not, do container-forming work first.

## The protocol
1. **Companion invitation and framing.**

- Companion: *"Tonight, [Participant], we'd like to invite you to tell us your story. Take whatever time you need. We'll hold this for you. As we listen, our job is to receive, not to process or respond — anything any of us has to say comes after, and you can ask for that or not. We have four questions that help shape what we're listening for: Where are the footprints of God in your life? Where are the wounds? Where are the battles? Where have there been victories and losses? You can move through them in any order, or let the chronology lead and let the four questions sit alongside as you go."*

- Participant: receives the invitation; takes a breath; decides where to begin.

- Discernment: if the participant looks unsettled by the framing, the Companion may add — *"there's no wrong way to do this; we'd rather hear what's alive than the polished version."* The framing's job is to free the participant into the telling, not to constrain them.

2. **Length decision.**

- Companion: name the length explicitly. Two core options: *the ten-minute version, told as if to someone who has never heard it before* (the harder and often more revealing form, because the participant discovers what they lead with), or *the full version, thirty minutes or more* (the slower, more chronological form). The choice depends on the group's time, the participant's preference, and where the group is in its life. Early-group story-tellings often run long; later cycles often choose the ten-minute form.

- Participant: chooses or assents to the form. May ask for the other form if the proposed one does not feel right.

- Discernment: if the participant defaults to the ten-minute form to avoid the longer telling, that pattern is itself information the Companion notes (without pressing during this telling). The longer form may be invited in a subsequent cycle.

3. **The telling.**

- Companion: become a witness yourself. Make eye contact with the teller; do not take notes during the telling (notes break the witness-pose); do not interrupt; do not ask clarifying questions; do not nod or murmur in ways that direct the teller toward what the listener wants to hear. Watch the room — if a listener begins to move into processing or speech, gently re-cue without disrupting the teller.

- Participant: tell the story. The four questions sit alongside the telling; the participant may name them explicitly ("this is a battles section") or let them be implicit. The participant may pause; the group holds the silence. The participant may move out of chronological order; the group follows.

- Discernment: notice without naming — what is the participant leading with? What are they getting to slowly? What are they leaving out? Where do they slow down, speed up, look away, become specific, become vague? This is information for the Companion to hold; it is not material to bring into the telling itself.

4. **The witness-discipline while the story is being told.**

- Companion: hold the room in witness mode. If a listener moves into processing — a clarifying question, a parallel story, a piece of theology — the Companion may say, gently, *"hold that; let [Participant] keep going,"* or simply re-direct attention back to the teller without verbal correction. If the listener persists, the Companion makes the witness-discipline explicit and brief: *"while [Participant] is telling, we're receiving; we'll have time after."* This is not punitive; it is structural.

- Group: receive. Make eye contact with the teller. Hold whatever arises in you without acting on it. Notice what stands out; you can name it in the reflection time after.

- Discernment: the witness-discipline is itself a competency that develops with reps. Early in a group's life it requires Companion-active maintenance; in mature groups it becomes the default mode and self-regulates.

5. **The participant signals completion.**

- Companion: do not press the participant to extend; do not press them to compress. The participant ends when the participant ends.

- Participant: signals completion — explicitly (*"that's where I'm going to stop tonight"*), or by trailing into silence and looking up to the group.

- Discernment: if the participant ends abruptly mid-thought in a way that suggests they want to stop but have not given themselves permission, the Companion may offer *"you can take more time if you have more; or we can hold here for tonight and pick up next time if you want."* Honor whatever the participant chooses.

6. **Group reflection (brief, in receiving mode).**

- Companion: open the reflection space with framing — *"if any of you wants to share what you heard or what stood out, please do; this is receiving, not responding."* Reflection is not pastoral counsel, not interpretation, not "here's what I think you should hear from your own story." It is what landed in the listener, named back to the room.

- Group: speak briefly if a piece of reflection wants to be spoken. Many tellings do not require reflection from every listener; sometimes silence is the right response. Reflection is offered to the teller as a gift of having been received, not as feedback or analysis.

- Discernment: the Companion holds the reflection time short and protects it from drifting into processing. If a listener begins to interpret the teller's story or to counsel them based on it, the Companion gently closes the reflection — *"thank you for that; let's hold here."* The brief reflection is sufficient.

7. **The teller's response (optional).**

- Companion: invite the teller to respond if they want — *"is there anything you want to say about what you've heard, or about how this telling felt?"* Optional; the teller may decline.

- Participant: respond briefly if there is something to say. Many tellers want to thank the group; some want to name what surprised them in their own telling; some want only to receive the reflection and not respond.

8. **Closing.**

- Companion: close the protocol with the standard V2.Exp8 marked-closing form (formally marked per the May 2026 Container refinement). A typical closing: *"Thank you, [Participant], for telling us your story. We've received it. We'll hold what we heard."* If the broader session-closing follows (with affirmations or blessing per A13 / V2.Exp9A), proceed to that; if this telling was the session's main content, close the container per the standard V2.Exp8 closing sequence.

- Group: receive the closing; do not extend it.

- Discernment: a closing affirmation or blessing may sometimes be appropriate in the V2.Exp9A pattern — *"what did the Lord show us in [Participant]'s story tonight, that we want to name back to him?"* — but only when the group is mature enough to deliver that without drifting into processing or counsel. In an early-forming group, the simpler closing is usually right.

## Closing and de-roling (if applicable)
**Not applicable.** Tell Your Story does not involve role-play; no figures are externalized; no participant holds a role for another. The de-roling protocol per A14 / V2.Exp8 is not invoked. The container closing uses the standard V2.Exp8 marked-closing form, not the reciprocal de-roling formula. (If the broader session that contained the story-telling included other role-play work, the de-roling for *that* work happens at its own closing; the story-telling itself does not generate a de-roling requirement.)

## Companion notes
The witness-discipline is the place to put your attention as you grow into leading this protocol. The structural moves — invite, frame the four questions, hold the room, close — are operationally simple and a Companion can do them adequately after a few reps. What separates a Tell Your Story session that does its work from one that fizzles is whether the group stayed in receiving mode while the man was telling. That depends on the Companion's own posture as much as on the framing language: if the Companion is in receiving mode during the telling — eye contact with the teller, no notes, no nodding-toward-where-they-want-the-teller-to-go, presence — the group typically follows. If the Companion drifts into processing or evaluative listening, the group follows that too. Lead by what you are doing, not by what you are saying.

The four-question framework's load-bearing question is **Footprints of God**. Make sure the framing reads that question first. *Where are the footprints of God in this life?* This is the question that pulls the story out of autobiography. The other three questions (Wounds, Battles, Victories) sit beside it and give the harder territory its own shape under the same overall frame. Do not let the four questions get treated as parallel-and-equal; the Footprints question is doing structural work the others are not.

A note on the cadence question (this is Founder-attention flag 3 in the Phase 2 entry): the practice expects retelling. Groups that hear each man's story once and treat the practice as complete develop thinner group-knowledge than groups that hear each man's story across years. A working default cadence: each man tells a substantial version (thirty-minute or longer) once during the Forming phase of the group; ten-minute retellings cycle through the group annually or at major lifecycle transitions; a new-member arrival is occasion for the new member to tell their story to the group and for the existing members to do a short re-telling so the new member knows them. Deviate from this default intentionally based on what the group needs.

A final note on the §5.1(c) gender-framing (Founder-attention flag 1 in the Phase 2 entry): the four-question framework as articulated here is the BoB form. FotH-women's-group and FotH-mixed-group Companions may want to adapt specific question-wordings — *Battles* in particular sits in an idiom that may not transfer cleanly. The framework structure — autobiography pulled into spiritual narrative by structured questions, told repeatedly in a witnessing group — appears to transfer well. The active development work on the FotH adaptation should be visible in subsequent revisions of this entry as it lands.
