# Exploration 0: The Christian Companion's Framework — Tool Import Discipline

## Why an Exploration 0
Many tools in this volume came from somewhere outside the Christian formation tradition before it entered our practice. The Grief Recovery Handbook came from secular grief work. TPM came from a particular evangelical lineage that had its own assumptions. Voice Dialogue came from depth psychology. Psychodrama came from Moreno. The Bilgere carpet-work recipe came from Mindell's Process-Oriented Psychology, which came from Jungian analytics. The 7×7 mortality contemplation came from existential-philosophy lineages with no Christian commitments at all. PROAPT was the closest to a native-Christian tool, and even it borrowed structural moves from Ignatian and Benedictine reading practice.

This is not a problem unless it is treated as one and then ignored. The problem is the opposite — pretending that any of these tools is a neutral instrument, an empty pipe through which Christian content can be poured without the pipe shaping what flows through it. The pipe has a shape. The shape was set by people who answered the questions "What is a person? What is wrong with us? What heals us? Whose work is it?" in particular ways. Those answers ride along with the tool, often invisibly, and they shape what the participant absorbs from experiencing the tool, even when the surface content is impeccably Christian.

The discipline of this Exploration is to bring the Christian framework explicitly to every tool: to name what aligns with the gospel, to adapt what does not, and to refuse what cannot be adapted without becoming something else. Powlison's three-step grid *frame/pattern/response* is the operational form of this work that I have found most clarifying, and the Phase 2 program's practitioner-level treatment of each tool applies the grid in detail. The grid asks three questions in sequence for each imported tool: What secular frame was the tool *developed under? What operational pattern does the tool **actually deploy** in practice? What is the right Christian response — receive, adapt, or refuse?*

## The Worked Example: Mortality-Contemplation Practices
I want to give one worked example in this volume, because the discipline is hard to grasp in the abstract. The mortality-contemplation practices the corpus deploys (the *memento mori* tradition from monastic sources; Heidegger's *Sein-**zum**-Tode* as it filtered into the Mankind Project's death-bed visualization; the 7×7 mortality contemplation Dmitri Bilgere developed; and beneath them, Heidegger's philosophical project of using finite mortality as the horizon against which authenticity is recovered) all share an operational form: the participant contemplates their own death in order to recover a clearer sense of what their life is actually for.

The operational force was real for me when I first experienced it and again as I led others through the process. People who do this work in a serious way come out of it with their priorities re-sorted, their pretenses thinned, their commitments clearer. The mechanism works. But the Heideggerian frame under which the practice was developed treats the participant's finite horizon as the ultimate reference point — death is the limit against which authenticity is gauged, and there is nothing beyond it that orders the meaning. That frame is not neutral. It quietly substitutes the participant's death for the master's return as the eschatological horizon under which the participant's life is to be ordered.

The Companion's work, when receiving this practice into Christian formation, is to re-frame it under an explicitly eschatological horizon: not death-as-final-limit but death-as-threshold-to-judgment, *to the master's return* (Luke 12:35–40), *to the judgment seat of Christ* (2 Cor 5:10), *to the day God has appointed* (Acts 17:31). The operational force of the contemplation is preserved; the source-tradition's metaphysical horizon is set aside; the reframing is the Formation Companion's work rather than the participant's; the participant receives the practice already reframed. That is what V2.Exp0 looks like at protocol depth for one tool. The Phase 2 entry on A15 (7×7 Process) and the Vol 5 Companion paper ([FC](../volume-5-references/source-pdfs/fc-formation-companion.pdf){: .pdf-popup data-pdf-label="FC — Formation Companion" }) give the full operational treatment.

## The Discipline in Brief
Phase 2 of the tool-import program — the practitioner-level treatment of each imported tool in Vol 5 — operates under this discipline throughout. Every tool entry names its source lineage, identifies what in the source frame requires adaptation or refusal, and states what the operational pattern looks like once it has been brought under the gospel framework. The Companion who deploys a tool without this discipline quietly smuggles its source framework into the formation work, with the participant absorbing the imported anthropology along with the imported technique. The Christian framework is the operating ground of the volume; every subsequent Exploration assumes it.

**Proposed Law (Tool Import Discipline): Tools imported from secular originals into Christian formation work are not neutral instruments; they carry the anthropology, epistemology, and account of agency embedded in their source frameworks. The ****Formation ****Companion brings the Christian framework to every tool — naming what aligns with the gospel, adapting what does not, and refusing what cannot be adapted. The Powlison three-step grid (frame / pattern / response) is the operational discipline that enacts this work. Tools deployed without this discipline quietly smuggle their source frameworks into Christian formation, with the participant absorbing the imported anthropology along with the imported technique.**
