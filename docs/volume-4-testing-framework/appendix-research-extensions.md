# Vol 4 Appendix: Research-Grade Extensions and Future Instruments

!!! warning "These testing protocols are proposed, not validated"

    Every instrument referenced in this Vol 4 framework — the Pilot Ready core (SIP, NRP, EWI), the extension instruments described below, and the protocols that integrate them — is a **proposed design**. The instruments themselves have not been individually validated through formal psychometric work; more importantly, the protocols **have not been tested as a group** in actual ministry settings. No pilot study has yet run the integrated framework end-to-end with real participants, real facilitators, real twelve-month timelines, and the cross-instrument convergence checks the framework predicts. The §1 framing — "drafts, ready for piloting and refinement" — applies to the framework as a whole, not only to individual instruments. Readers and future pilot teams should treat the Vol 4 protocol as a thoughtful design ready for testing, not as a battle-tested measurement program. Findings from any pilot use should be expected to revise both the individual instruments and the protocol's structural choices.

(A separate "Appendix A" is referenced elsewhere in Vol 4 — in §3b, §3d, and §5 — as the home for the full draft instrument items themselves, e.g., the actual PSFA items, the actual STI-A items. Those drafts existed in the original Word manuscript but were not pulled through into the published markdown; that publication remains a future task and is unrelated to this appendix. This appendix is *not* the same Appendix A those sections refer to; it is a separate disposition list about which instruments are core versus extension.)

## Why this appendix exists

The Vol 4 testing protocol is built around a **Pilot Ready Protocol** — a three-instrument core (the Stage Integration Profile, the Narrative Reflection Protocol, and the External Witness Interview) designed so that a real pastor, facilitator, or small-group leader in a real church can actually run it without being overwhelmed by assessment load. The §4e section already insists that the facilitator is the single most important variable in whether a PROAPT group produces genuine formation; that claim is operationally meaningful only if the protocol leaves the facilitator the time, attention, and presence the role requires.

The instruments collected here were developed earlier in the Vol 4 design as part of a broader twelve-instrument suite. They remain operationally sound *as proposed designs* (see the warning above) and are preserved here for facilitators, research teams, doctoral-level studies, or future programs that want deeper instrumentation in specific dimensions once the framework moves into actual pilot use. They are not part of the Pilot Ready core. Adding any of them to a real-world protocol is an explicit choice with a known cost in facilitator and participant load; the decision should be made deliberately and with awareness of what the core three instruments already capture.

The transition from the earlier full-suite design to the Pilot Ready core happened in three published passes:

- **Pass A (landed).** This appendix was created as an index. The canonical descriptions of the eight extension instruments remained in §3 and §4; this appendix cross-referenced them with Pilot Ready disposition notes.
- **Pass B (landed).** The Stage Integration Profile (SIP — both Individual and Group variants) was published as a new core instrument in [§3d](section-3-individual-spiritual-formation-testing-protocol.md#3d-the-three-assessment-instruments) and §4 (Group SIP), alongside the existing twelve instruments. New Outcome Variable 5 (Individual SIP Trajectory) was added to §3c; new Group Outcome Variable 6 (Group SIP Trajectory) was added to §4d.
- **Pass C (landed in this Vol 4 revision).** §3 and §4 are now restructured around the Pilot Ready three-instrument core. The canonical descriptions of the eight extension instruments have migrated **into this appendix**; §3 and §4 retain brief stub references back here. The Image of God 4Rs is now folded into the NRP as a recurring prompt set. The Tell Rate is kept at the group level as a single weekly facilitator check rather than as part of the multi-variable Session Observation Protocol. The §4e Facilitator PSFA is superseded by Facilitator SIP. The published Vol 4 protocol now runs the Pilot Ready three-instrument core by default; the instruments below are available as deliberate extensions.

## The eight extension instruments

### PSFA — Personal Spiritual Formation Assessment (AT-stage portion)

**Pilot Ready disposition:** superseded by SIP-Individual in the Pilot Ready core (the AT-stage section of the PSFA is replaced by the SIP; the Image of God 4Rs section of the PSFA is folded into the NRP as a recurring prompt set).

**Canonical description (migrated from §3d in Pass C):**

The PSFA is a 25-item self-report instrument that assesses the participant's current position on the Affective Taxonomy and their self-perceived growth in the Image of God 4Rs. It was originally the primary quantitative instrument of the individual testing protocol. The PSFA borrows the *layered self-report architecture* of Hall's (2017) Spiritual Transformation Inventory — a primary construct partitioned into sections, with behaviorally-anchored items in each — but applies that architecture to a developmental progression (the Affective Taxonomy's five stages) rather than to a domain map (Hall's five connection domains). The first section of the PSFA presents stage-characteristic behavioral statements at each of the five Affective Taxonomy stages; the second section presents items in each of the four R-dimensions (four items each). The two structures (Hall's five domains and the AT's five stages) are not substitutable in content — they describe different things — but Hall's instrument-design pattern is the closest validated reference for what the PSFA is trying to do.

The PSFA was designed for administration at baseline, six months, and twelve months. Participants respond to each item on a five-point scale from "This does not describe me" to "This describes me consistently and habitually." The five-point scale was chosen deliberately to discourage the middle response and to force a direction. The items are written as behavioral and experiential descriptions — not as belief statements ("I believe scripture is true") but as formation statements ("When I read scripture, I find myself making specific changes to my behavior in response"). This is consistent with the Affective Taxonomy's focus on the affective dimension of learning, which is always manifest in behavior and disposition, not just in cognition.

**When to add this instrument:** when a study needs the original PSFA-format AT classification for comparability to earlier work, or when a research design wants a Likert-format alternative to the SIP's forced-choice distribution. The SIP and PSFA-AT-section are answering different questions: PSFA classifies the participant at their highest consistent stage; SIP profiles the participant across all five stages with the integration check built in. Studies running both produce richer cross-instrument data — useful for psychometric work on the SIP itself.

### STI-A — Scripture Trust Index (Adapted)

**Pilot Ready disposition:** optional / research-grade. The SIP captures trust-in-scripture as an aspect of Dimensions 1 (engagement with scripture) and 3 (how scripture shapes decisions); a dedicated trust instrument is not part of the Pilot Ready core.

**Canonical description (migrated from §3d in Pass C):**

The STI-A is a 12-item self-report instrument adapted from Campbell et al.'s (2021) epistemic trust measure for use in a Christian spiritual formation context. The original instrument measures trust in communicated knowledge in a therapeutic relationship; the LotS adaptation replaces the therapist with scripture as the trusted source and recalibrates the items to reflect the specific dimensions of trust in the biblical text: trust in its reliability, in its personal applicability, in its authority over life decisions, and in its sufficiency as a guide.

The STI-A measures only the trust dimension of Campbell et al.'s three-dimensional construct (trust, mistrust, credulity), because trust is the dimension that maps directly onto the Affective Taxonomy's progression. Credulity — uncritical acceptance of anything claimed in the name of scripture — is not a formation goal; it is a spiritual vulnerability. The STI-A includes two items designed to detect credulity as a possible ceiling effect: a participant who scores very high on trust but also high on credulity items is not at Stage 5 of the Affective Taxonomy; they may be at an immature Stage 2 or 3 where enthusiasm has not yet been tested.

The STI-A is administered at baseline, six months, and twelve months. A meaningful positive change is defined as a movement of one or more points on the overall trust scale, accompanied by qualitative narrative evidence (in the NRP entries) of the specific experiences or encounters with scripture that drove the change. Score change without narrative evidence is recorded but not interpreted as meaningful stage-level progress; the LotS framework predicts that genuine trust development is always associated with specific moments of hearing, specific encounters with the word that cut through in a new way.

The STI-A score also serves as a check on the Affective Taxonomy stage assessment. The two measures should move in the same direction: a person who is progressing on the Affective Taxonomy should show increasing scripture trust, and a person who shows increasing scripture trust should be progressing on the Affective Taxonomy. Where the two measures diverge — a person showing behavioral stage progression but declining scripture trust, for example — the discrepancy is a research flag that invites qualitative investigation. Divergence of this kind is also one of the patterns the [§2 integration reading](section-2-testing-framework.md#reading-the-affective-taxonomy-as-integration-not-replacement) predicts: stage-characteristic behavior hardening into form while the receiving-and-responding life that should accompany it has stalled is the trust-dimension signature of stage-N collapse. Under the Pilot Ready core, the SIP's native closure-flag detects the same pattern directly; STI-A-vs-SIP divergence is the additional triangulation an extension study can use.

**When to add this instrument:** when a study or pilot wants a dedicated trust-in-scripture measurement that runs independently of the SIP's broader profile. The SIP captures trust-in-scripture as an aspect of Dimensions 1 (engagement with scripture) and 3 (how scripture shapes decisions); a dedicated STI-A is useful when the trust dimension specifically is the research target — e.g., interventions designed to address scripture-trust deficits, longitudinal trust-development studies, or comparative work against Campbell et al.'s original instrument.

### SDFL — Spiritual Disciplines Frequency Log

**Pilot Ready disposition:** optional / research-grade. The Pilot Ready core does not require frequency data because the formation reading depends primarily on the qualitative texture the NRP captures, not on practice counts.

**Canonical description (migrated from §3d in Pass C):**

The SDFL is a simple weekly log — one page per month — on which the participant records the frequency and approximate duration of five categories of spiritual practice: personal bible reading and study, personal prayer, participation in the PROAPT small group, participation in corporate worship, and acts of service or giving. The SDFL is designed to take less than five minutes per week to complete; if it takes longer, it has been over-designed.

The SDFL is the least theoretically interesting of the extension instruments — frequency counts alone tell you almost nothing about formation. But frequency data becomes interpretively rich when correlated with the SIP profiles and the NRP narratives. The SDFL answers the question: under what conditions of practice did this person's formation occur? A participant who shows significant stage progression with low and irregular discipline frequency is an interesting anomaly that invites investigation. A participant whose discipline frequency is high but whose stage progression is flat is pointing toward the most important distinction in the LotS framework: the difference between doing the practices and allowing the word to land in the heart.

**When to add this instrument:** when a study wants quantitative practice-frequency data that can be correlated with formation outcomes. The SDFL answers questions of the form "did formation occur under conditions of high or low discipline frequency?" — useful for studies investigating the relationship between practice intensity and stage progression, or for participants whose own self-understanding benefits from explicit frequency tracking.

### GSFP — Group Spiritual Formation Profile

**Pilot Ready disposition:** optional / research-grade. The Pilot Ready core does not include the GSFP because the individual SIPs at baseline / midpoint / endpoint contain the same individual-stage information in profile form, and the Group SIP captures the collective-culture reading the GSFP was partly designed to inform.

**Canonical description (migrated from §4c in Pass C):**

The Group Spiritual Formation Profile is a visual mapping tool that shows the distribution of individual Affective Taxonomy stages within the group at any given assessment point. It is updated quarterly by the facilitator, based on their Stage assessment of each participant, and serves as an additional instrument for tracking the group's collective development over the twelve-month period.

The GSFP is not a ranking of group members — it is never shared with the group in a way that identifies individuals by stage. Its purpose is to give the facilitator and research coordinator a picture of the group's composition and how it is evolving. A GSFP that shows, at Month 3, three participants at Stage 2 and two at Stage 3, and that shows, at Month 12, those same two at Stage 3 and three at Stage 3 with two approaching Stage 4, is telling a story about collective formation that no single participant's individual arc could tell alone.

| Assessment Point | Stage Distribution (example) | Group Affective Taxonomy Stage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Baseline (Month 1) | 2× Stage 1 \| 3× Stage 2 \| 1× Stage 3 \| 1× Stage 4 | GATS: Stage 2 |
| Mid-point (Month 6) | 0× Stage 1 \| 2× Stage 2 \| 3× Stage 3 \| 1× Stage 4 | GATS: Stage 3 (emerging) |
| Endpoint (Month 12) | 0× Stage 1 \| 1× Stage 2 \| 2× Stage 3 \| 3× Stage 4 \| 1× Stage 5 | GATS: Stage 3–4 |

The example GSFP above illustrates the kind of movement the LotS protocol predicts: a net upward shift across the group, with the most advanced participants continuing to grow and the least advanced participants moving into genuine engagement (Stage 2 to Stage 3 transition) over the course of the year. It also illustrates a realistic complexity: not every participant moves at the same rate, and at Month 12, the group still has one participant at Stage 2. That participant's presence in the group is not a failure of the protocol. A Stage 2 participant embedded in a Stage 3 group culture is exposed, week after week, to the normalized behaviors of the next stage, which is exactly the formation environment the LotS framework predicts will eventually catalyze their own transition.

!!! info "Instrument cross-reference — NCD's *3 Colors of Community*"

    A specific external-validity check is worth naming here as a concrete action item for the multi-site research design. Schwarz's *The 3 Colors of Community* (ChurchSmart, 2012) includes an empirically-developed communal test instrument that assesses small-group health across seven communal qualities — mapped, in Schwarz's framework, against the seven deadly sins as their corresponding blockage counterparts. The overlap with Vol 2's emotional-knots framework is not incidental: where Vol 2 identifies the ways individual affective distortions obstruct formation, Schwarz's seven communal qualities identify the collective-level analogs — the patterns of group dysfunction that prevent a community from becoming the formation environment it is meant to be.

!!! info "Vol 2 connection — Standing Interior Agreements (V2.Exp2C)"

    Vol 2 introduces V2.Exp2C (Standing Interior Agreements) as a sibling claim under V2.Exp2, naming the upstream binding agent that produces and re-produces emotional knots across multiple blockage-types over years and decades. The *agreement* is operationally distinct from a *knot*: a knot is what the participant is carrying; an agreement is what the participant has committed to carrying. A single standing agreement may produce grief knots, shame knots, fear knots, anger knots, and sin-channel behaviors all from the same underlying binding.

    For the Vol 4 testing program this matters in two specific ways. **First,** the GSFP and STI-A measurement scaffolding tracks knot-level patterns; V2.Exp2C suggests the LotS protocol may need a complementary instrument for agreement-level patterns, since knot-level interventions may show transient effects (six-month or twelve-month re-formation of cleared patterns) when an upstream agreement is still in force. **Second,** the Schwarz communal-qualities instrument's identification of group-dysfunction patterns may map not only to Vol 2's knot framework (individual affective distortion) but also to V2.Exp2C's agreement framework (the collective standing agreements a group has entered about who they are, what they are permitted to be, how they are loyal to the group system). Group-level standing agreements are an open hypothesis the multi-site testing program could investigate.

Administering both the GSFP and the NCD 3 Colors of Community communal instrument to the same groups in any multi-site study would allow the research team to assess convergent validity between the two instruments. If a group that the GSFP identifies as formation-blocked also scores low on the relevant NCD communal quality, that convergence would provide evidence that the two instruments are measuring related constructs from different angles. If they diverge, that divergence is equally informative — it would indicate that the GSFP is capturing something the NCD instrument misses, or vice versa. Either result advances the measurement program. This is a low-cost addition to the research design and a high-return one.

**When to add this instrument:** when a research team wants the individual-distribution view of the group's formation alongside the group-culture view. Group SIP measures the group's collective culture profile across the five AT stages; the GSFP measures which individuals are at which stages at a given point. These are complementary cuts. The GSFP is also useful when the facilitator wants a quick visual reference for how the group's composition is evolving across the twelve-month arc.

### SOP — Session Observation Protocol

**Pilot Ready disposition:** optional / research-grade. The Pilot Ready core replaces the SOP with a single-question weekly Tell-Rate check kept at the group level (see [§4d Group OV2](section-4-small-group-spiritual-formation-testing.md#4d-group-level-outcome-variables)). The SOP is the single largest facilitator-load item in the previous full-suite protocol — completing it weekly is the kind of task that competes with facilitator presence during the session itself.

**Canonical description (migrated from §4f in Pass C):**

The Session Observation Protocol (SOP) is a structured one-page checklist that the facilitator completes after every PROAPT session. It takes five to ten minutes and is designed to capture the formation-relevant dynamics of the session in a form that can be aggregated across sessions and analyzed over time.

The SOP tracks six session-level variables: (1) the number of participants who engaged substantively in the Observe step — asking factual questions, making connections between the text and prior sessions; (2) the number who shared personal applications in the Apply step that reflected genuine personal cost or conviction; (3) the number who reported a Tell from the previous week; (4) the number who named a specific person for this week's Tell; (5) any significant emotional moment in the session — a moment of visible conviction, repentance, or breakthrough that the facilitator identifies as a potential stage transition indicator; and (6) the facilitator's overall rating of the session's depth of formation engagement on a five-point scale.

The SOP data builds into a session-by-session formation record for each group. Plotted over twelve months, the SOP scores tell a story: groups that begin with low Apply and Tell scores and gradually develop richer personal engagement are tracking the Stage 2-to-3 movement that the LotS protocol predicts. Groups whose SOP scores are flat or declining after Month 3 are signaling that something in the group's formation environment is not working, and the qualitative data from the facilitator's supervision notes should be examined to understand what.

**When to add this instrument:** when a research team or doctoral study wants session-by-session formation data that can be aggregated across twelve months. Studies wanting the richer session-level data should add the full SOP back deliberately, with explicit attention to whether the facilitator has the time and attention to complete it without compromising presence in the room.

### CSFA — Collective Spiritual Formation Assessment

**Pilot Ready disposition:** optional / research-grade. The Group SIP largely covers the same operational territory but organized around the five AT stages with the integration check built in.

**Canonical description (migrated from §4g in Pass C):**

In addition to the individual SIP profiles and the group's Group SIP profile, the CSFA is a brief Collective Spiritual Formation Assessment administered to the group as a whole at three formal assessment points: baseline, Month 6, and Month 12. The CSFA asks participants to evaluate the group itself — not their own formation, but what they observe in the group's shared culture and collective life with scripture.

The CSFA has twelve items organized into three domains. The first domain assesses the group's corporate engagement with scripture: do participants experience the group's reading and discussion of the word as qualitatively different from reading alone? Do they find that the group hears things they would not have heard individually? The second domain assesses the group's corporate trust and safety: do participants feel genuinely known, genuinely safe to share their formation journey, and genuinely accountable to the group's collective discernment? The third domain assesses the group's outward orientation: is the formation that happens in the room crossing the threshold into the participants' daily lives, relationships, and communities?

The CSFA is administered anonymously, and results are shared with the group in aggregate at the Month 6 and Month 12 check-in sessions. This shared feedback is itself a formation practice: a group that can look honestly at its own CSFA scores — noting where its corporate life is strong and where it is thin — and discuss those results with humility and curiosity, is exhibiting Stage 3 behaviors at the group level. A group that receives CSFA feedback defensively, or that cannot discuss its collective weaknesses without becoming conflictual, is showing the facilitator exactly what formation work remains to be done.

**When to add this instrument:** when a study wants a complementary cut on group culture organized by domain rather than by AT stage. CSFA's three-domain organization is useful when a study wants to track corporate-engagement / trust-safety / outward-orientation as separately analyzable variables. Alternating quarterly administration of Group SIP and CSFA (rather than both at every point) is one way to retain the CSFA's information without compounding survey load on participants.

### GCS — Group Cohesion Scale

**Pilot Ready disposition:** optional / research-grade. The Pilot Ready core does not require the GCS because Group SIP Dimensions 3 (welcoming new voices), 4 (prayer life), and 5 (conflict and correction) cover the operational territory the GCS measures, organized within the same instrument and integration-checked.

**Canonical description (migrated from §4d Group OV2 in Pass C):**

The Group Cohesion Scale is an adapted instrument drawn from the group therapy literature and modified for a spiritual formation context. It addresses Stage 3 formation — the transition from intellectual engagement to personal commitment — which requires a specific quality of group environment: enough trust and safety for a participant to share a personal conviction, acknowledge a sin, or report a costly behavioral change without fear of judgment or embarrassment. A group that remains at a surface level of politeness — where no one ever takes a real personal risk — is not a Stage 3 group, regardless of what the participants' individual SIP scores say.

Group cohesion is assessed quarterly using the GCS. The instrument asks participants to rate the degree to which they feel genuinely known by the group, safe to share honestly, and supported in their formation journey. A rising GCS score over the twelve-month period is both an outcome of the formation process and a precondition for the deeper stages of formation — the two are mutually reinforcing.

The cohesion variable also functions as an early warning system. A group whose GCS scores are declining or flat at Month 6 is unlikely to produce Stage 3-level formation in its remaining six months without a significant intervention. The facilitator's observation notes and any SOP data from this period should be examined carefully to identify what is preventing the development of the psychological safety that Stage 3 formation requires.

A complementary epistemic-trust pair of items is sometimes added to the quarterly GCS: one item asking whether the participant trusts the group's collective discernment as a source of guidance for their own life decisions, and one item asking whether they have acted on something the group discerned together in the past quarter. Movement on these two items over twelve months is a group-level indicator of the developing communal trust that the LotS framework predicts will emerge in a maturing PROAPT group, drawing on Campbell et al.'s (2021) epistemic trust model.

**When to add this instrument:** when a study wants a validated, externally-grounded cohesion measure independent of the AT integration framing. The GCS connects the Vol 4 protocol to the broader group-therapy research literature and provides a comparison point for spiritual-formation small groups against secular therapeutic groups.

### Facilitator PSFA

**Pilot Ready disposition:** superseded by Facilitator SIP in the Pilot Ready core. The Facilitator SIP is simply the SIP-Individual completed by the facilitator on themselves at the start and end of the study; the integration-check advantage it carries — flagging the closed Stage 4 facilitator that a single-stage classification would miss — is precisely what the [§4e](section-4-small-group-spiritual-formation-testing.md#4e-the-facilitator-as-a-critical-variable) integrated-vs-closed caveat names as operationally consequential.

**Canonical description:**

The Facilitator PSFA is the PSFA-format version of the facilitator's own AT stage assessment, documented at the start of the study as a formal independent variable in group-outcome analysis. The original protocol treated the facilitator's Affective Taxonomy stage as a documented constant held throughout the analysis of group outcomes. The prediction — and this is an Open Trail worth testing formally — was that groups led by Stage 4 facilitators would show significantly greater net stage progression over twelve months than groups led by Stage 3 facilitators, even when all other variables (group composition, church context, PROAPT fidelity) were controlled.

**When to add this instrument:** when a study wants the original PSFA-format facilitator instrument for comparability to earlier work or to the existing literature. The Facilitator SIP supersedes the Facilitator PSFA in the Pilot Ready core because the SIP's profile architecture surfaces the integrated-vs-closed pattern directly; a closed Stage 4 facilitator would PSFA-rate as Stage 4 while Facilitator SIP would show the asymmetric profile (high D/E, starved A) that flags closure before it propagates into the group culture.

### OT-5 Supplementary Interview

**Pilot Ready disposition:** situational / research-grade. This instrument is triggered by events, not by schedule.

**Canonical description (referenced in [§4h Phase 2](section-4-small-group-spiritual-formation-testing.md#4h-the-testing-timeline-for-small-groups)):**

A brief structured interview triggered when any participant experiences a significant formation breakthrough during the study — a dramatic conviction, sudden reorganization of values, unexpected surge of higher-stage behavior. The interview is conducted within two weeks of the event and documents the conditions that preceded it: PROAPT engagement, personal circumstances, prayer life, relational context. This interview data is supplementary qualitative data for the Open Trail 5 question of what precedes instantaneous or dramatic heart change.

**When to add this instrument:** this instrument is in the Pilot Ready protocol contingently — if a triggering event occurs, the facilitator should document it via the OT-5 protocol regardless of whether the rest of the extension suite is in use. The instrument is in this appendix rather than embedded in the main protocol description because most studies will not encounter the triggering events; when they do, the protocol is available here.

## How to use this appendix

The recommended practice for any pilot or study:

1. Run the Pilot Ready core (SIP-Individual + NRP + EWI for individuals; Group SIP + Tell Rate + aggregated EWI for groups) without modification. This is the minimum viable protocol designed to be runnable in real church contexts by real facilitators.
2. Identify the specific research question or pastoral need that an extension instrument would address.
3. Add only the extension instruments that address that specific need.
4. Track facilitator load and participant survey load as protocol variables; the addition of any extension instrument is expected to affect both.

The Pilot Ready core was designed to produce sound formation data on its own. Each extension instrument adds a specific capacity at a specific cost. The point of this appendix is not to discourage use of the extensions but to make the cost-benefit decision deliberate.
